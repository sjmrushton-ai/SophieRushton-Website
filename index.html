About

Sophie Rushton creates living cartographies of nature, memory, mathematics and time.

My practice explores pattern as a living system. Inspired by fungi, rivers, roots, cells and Indigenous pattern languages that encode journeys, I investigate how simple marks evolve into complex structures. Rather than illustrating nature, I seek to create works that behave like nature.

Each work begins with tiny cellular marks that gradually elongate, branch, split, reconnect and dissolve. Guided by repetition, variation and growth, the composition is allowed to emerge rather than being predetermined. The process mirrors the quiet intelligence of living systems.

The marks feel cellular, fungal and river-like, almost as though they are making decisions. They imply time because they appear to have been laid down one after another, recording the history of their own formation. Every brushstroke becomes a moment. Every cluster becomes a memory of a previous decision.

Underlying these organic processes is an exploration of the mathematics of emergence, the idea that extraordinary complexity can arise from remarkably simple rules. My work draws inspiration from the Fibonacci sequence, the Golden Ratio, branching systems, recursion, fractals, network theory and higher-dimensional geometry. These universal structures appear throughout nature, revealing mathematics not as something separate from the natural world, but as one of its deepest languages.

I think of pattern not as decoration, but as a way of carrying knowledge. Across cultures, pattern has preserved ecological understanding, encoded journeys, told stories and expressed relationships between people and the natural world. Rather than reproducing historical traditions, I draw inspiration from this philosophy, creating contemporary works that explore pattern as a living record of movement, memory and transformation.

Working across painting, ink, embroidery, print and textiles, each piece is built slowly and intentionally. Repetition becomes both a way of making and a way of thinking. The finished work is not simply an image. It is the visible trace of an unfolding process.

As a graduate of the London College of Fashion, University of the Arts London, my background in textiles has shaped the way I think about pattern as both a visual language and a material practice. Early in my career I worked as an embroidery designer for British couturier Bruce Oldfield, contributing hand-drawn embroidery designs for Queen Camilla's Coronation gown. Today, my practice has expanded beyond fashion into fine art, where I continue to explore the relationship between craftsmanship, mathematics, ecology and cultural knowledge.

A Thoughtful Way of Making

Sustainability is fundamental to my practice.

If my work explores time, then the way it is made must also honour time. I believe meaningful objects deserve an equally thoughtful process, one that values care over speed, longevity over excess, and connection over consumption.

Wherever possible, I work with surplus fabrics, responsibly sourced materials and small-scale production. Every decision is made with consideration, both for the people each artwork is created for and for the resources from which it is made.

I see sustainability not simply as reducing waste, but as creating objects with lasting value. Pieces that are lived with, repaired, treasured and passed from one generation to the next.

In many cultures, textiles and patterns have long been vessels for memory, knowledge and identity. They were created to endure, carrying stories through time rather than existing for a single season. I hope my work continues this tradition by creating pieces that invite people to slow down, look closely and form lasting relationships with the objects they live alongside.

Because the most enduring objects are those that tell a story worth keeping.

“Each work is a living cartography, grown through mathematics, shaped by nature, carrying memory through time, and made to endure.”
